How Peptides Signal

The receptor family Kobilka won a Nobel for — and why ~1 in 3 drugs targets it

Peptides mostly talk to receptors

Most signaling peptides work by fitting a specific receptor — like a key cut for one lock. The single most important receptor family for peptide drugs is the G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR). GPCRs are the target of an estimated one-third of all approved drugs.1

Why Kobilka's Nobel matters

For decades GPCRs were understood only indirectly. Brian Kobilka (Stanford) captured the first high-resolution structures of a GPCR — including the receptor caught in the act of signaling — earning the 2012 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.1 Seeing the actual shape is what lets chemists design molecules that fit.

Agonist vs antagonist

  • An agonist binds and activates the receptor (semaglutide is a GLP-1 receptor agonist).
  • An antagonist binds and blocks it.

"Partial" and "biased" agonists activate only some of a receptor's downstream signals — an active frontier in making drugs with fewer side effects.

The payoff: once you know a peptide's receptor, you can predict a lot — what it does, what it interacts with, and why two peptides on the same receptor behave alike.
