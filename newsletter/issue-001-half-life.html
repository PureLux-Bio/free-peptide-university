Native GLP-1 lasts about two minutes. Semaglutide lasts a week.
Same core signal. The difference is not a stronger molecule — it is a piece of chemistry called half-life engineering, and it is the quiet reason the biggest drugs of the decade can be taken once a week instead of every few minutes.
The trick is elegant: attach a fatty-acid chain so the peptide reversibly grabs onto albumin, the most abundant protein in your blood. Hidden on albumin, it slips past the enzymes that would normally clear it in minutes. That single idea — plus a few cousins like PEGylation and DAC linkers — explains why “No-DAC” and “DAC” versions of the same peptide behave completely differently.
